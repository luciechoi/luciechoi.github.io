Project Overview

Created as part of the creative drawing task for Stanford's CS248A, the above artwork was drawn in SVG (Scalable Vector Graphics), using Inkscape Bézier curves, Sketch, Adobe Illustrator, and reference images. It was converted into discrete polygon meshes, which were then rendered into a raster image using a custom-built software rasterizer.

Technical Details

The rasterizer supports the following features:

  • Generalized Line Drawing: Modified Bresenham's algorithm for arbitrary slopes in all four quadrants using floating-point arithmetic.
  • Triangle Drawing: Rasterizes triangles using 2D bounding box and triangle-edge rule.
  • Anti-Aliasing using Supersampling (SSAA): Multi-rate sub-pixel sample buffers to eliminate jagged edges, using box or truncated Gaussian filter.
  • Transforms & Hierarchical Modeling: Homogeneous coordinate matrix transformations supporting nested group hierarchies alongside interactive panning and zooming.
  • Texturing & Sampling: Nearest-neighbor and bilinear texture filtering.
  • Alpha Compositing: Alpha blending for rendering semi-transparent elements.

Behind The Piece

Motivation

This work is dedicated to my grandmother, who passed away while I was working on this project, during Lunar New Year in 2025. Unfortunately, I couldn't fly to Korea to say goodbye.

The drawing depicts a cherished childhood memory of mine during Lunar New Year (설날 / Seollal). In Korean tradition, families gather in traditional clothing (한복 / Hanbok) to perform 세배 (Sebae), a deep ceremonial bow to the elders, expressing gratitude and receiving warm blessings for the year ahead.

Story of my grandmother

Being unable to be there in person made me reflect for the first time on the sheer weight of history she carried.

Born during the Japanese colonization, she lived through the devastation of the Korean War and the heartache of losing her brothers. She would tell us about her childhood — albeit very rarely — and how she fled south alone.

The day after her passing, I pictured a little girl hiding in a barn, trembling, where she spent a night alone next to the dead body of a baby. She said she was even hiding from American soldiers, South Korea's allies, because it was simply not safe to get noticed as a young woman in such times. The horror of wars, something that goes untold in textbooks.

And the story of meeting my grandfather, who also fled South, and how he passed away when my father was young, raising the two sons by herself.

History, Religion, and Freedom

Global tragedies are not just chapters in history books; they leave deep, indelible marks on human lives that echo across generations, shaping my father's life and ultimately my own.

On New Year's Day, we bow to our ancestors. However, shortly after, we also pray to God and read the Bible together. At night, when it's hard to fall asleep, my parents listen to sermons of Buddhist monks on Youtube, and I, the ambient sound of temples.

I never realized that this, such ordinary scenery in Korea, may seem bizarre to some people.

However, with the context of history, how my grandmother was raised by Catholic nuns after the war, how my mom went to an all-girls Christian school, and how we seek comfort and connections to our culture in Buddhism and shamanism, it all makes sense.

Here I am today, in the United States, reminded of the vision that Kim Gu (김구) had, a leader of the Korean independence movement. He dreamed of a country where various religions and ideologies can freely coexist, and such a vision is now a reality. It's a reality in my very own life, yet one denied to my relatives in the North, whom I never met.

And now, 70 years after the war, these first-hand memories are slowly slipping away, finding their way into peace... only to be recounted and passed down by the generations to come.
